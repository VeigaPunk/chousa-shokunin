837: Objectives are ‘active statements’ that describe how the review is going to answer your review question/s (Farrugia et al. 2010). The objectives and questions should align to clearly demonstrate what the review will achieve and how it will achieve it. The development of a review question is a critical step in the conduct of a scoping review. It not only determines if a scoping review is the right approach but also then informs the foundation for the eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening, extraction, analysis, presentation and findings of the review. If a review question is unclear, the review will lack coherence. Table 4 provides alignment between the objective, review questions and review population, concept and context.
838: A scoping review question is broader than those in systematic reviews and can be seen as hypothesis-generating, whereas a systematic review is hypothesis-testing (Tricco et al. 2018b). JBI suggests utilising the PCC (population, concept and context) approach to formulate review questions for scoping reviews. Use of the PCC approach clearly identifies the focus and context of a scoping review, further enabling utility and transparency for the reader. Some scoping review questions benefit from one or more subquestions that delve into attributes of the population, concept and context and help operationalise the review.
839: If the concept of equity is relevant to your review question, it is important to consider whether your scoping review will have an equity focus in its entirety or as a secondary question or objective. For example, a scoping review examining gender equity (Tricco et al. 2024) as its primary emphasis versus a scoping review examining machine learning tools in hospital settings with a secondary objective to examine equity issues in this area (Tricco et al. 2023a).
840: For some scoping reviews, it may be that one of the elements of the PCC is not required, depending on the question and objective. For others, there may be a compelling argument to use a different question format (such as PICO or Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome [PECO]), such as when performing a structured analysis of existing research or research gaps, or as a precursor to a systematic review. If using a PICO/PECO format when conducting a scoping review as a precursor for a systematic review, there will be greater alignment in the eligibility criteria and article screening/selection between the scoping review and subsequent systematic review/s.

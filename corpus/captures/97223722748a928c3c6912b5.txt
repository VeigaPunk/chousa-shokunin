 10.2.4 Developing and aligning the eligibility criteria with the objective/s and question/s
 
 

 
 By Carolyn Phillips 
 
 6 min 
 
 Add a reaction
 






 



 



 



 
 
 
 
 Having a clear alignment between the objective and review question creates clarity when developing the eligibility criteria. To create the eligibility criteria, each element of the PCC approach is broken down into a further description of what the review will include and exclude. The eligibility criteria are often referred to throughout the screening process and play an important role in establishing clarity for all reviewers. It is suggested to be as detailed as possible to assist in the conduct of your review. In Table 4, we have provided six examples of scoping reviews and the alignment between objectives, review questions, population, concept, context and types of studies.


 Population 
 Important characteristics of the population/participants should be detailed, including age and other qualifying criteria. If any equity-related demographics will be examined (e.g. age, economic country level, gender), this should be clarified. Review teams should examine the PROGRESS-Plus framework, which was developed to consider health equity within reviews. This framework should be considered along with context in relation to how the team is then able to consider equity-focused analysis and findings within its scoping review.
 Concept 
 The core concept examined by the scoping review should be clearly articulated to guide the scope and breadth of the inquiry. This may include elements that would be detailed in a standard systematic review, such as the ‘interventions’, ‘phenomena of interest’ and/or ‘outcomes’. If the concept is related to equity, this should be clarified.


 Context 
 The ‘context’ element of a scoping review will vary depending on the objective/s and question/s of the review. The context should be clearly defined and may include, but is not limited to, consideration of equity issues, such as cultural factors, geographic location and/or specific racial or gender-based interests. In some cases, context may also encompass details about the specific setting (such as acute care, primary health care or the community when considering health care topics).


 Types of evidence sources 
 In scoping reviews, we often use the term ‘evidence source’ or ‘document’ as opposed to study or article. This is because a scoping review may aim to include different types of articles/reports/documents beyond empirical research studies. As such, a scoping review can include any type of evidence source that is pertinent to answering your question. This is not limited to only peer-reviewed articles, and may include theses, websites, books, reports, blogs, manuals, etc. The types of evidence sources included within your scoping review should be clearly described within the protocol and report. If only looking at empirical studies, you may call this section ‘types of studies’


 Can I include evidence syntheses in my scoping review? 
 Other forms of evidence synthesis can be included within a scoping review. Review teams should consider how and why they would include this form of evidence. The benefits for this include the chance of capturing the full breadth of the topic landscape, the potential to recommend overviews (umbrella reviews) and the opportunity to identify additional reports to be considered for eligibility in your scoping review. However, you may need to assess if double-counting will be a concern. We discuss the issue of double-counting in scoping reviews later on in this chapter.

 The objective of this scoping review is to examine and map the range of vocational rehabilitation (VR) available for emergency services (EMS) personnel
 Which types of VR interventions have been reported for police, firefighter and ambulance/paramedic personnel from developed countries?

 What are the characteristics of the VR (including, but not limited to, physical interventions, psychological interventions or mixed content interventions) reported for police, firefighter and EMS personnel from developed countries?

 Systematic reviews, textual evidence papers, professional and government reports, guidelines and unpublished material
